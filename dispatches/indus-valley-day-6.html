Temples of the Mind: Decoding Harappan Faith and Order

Sorting hard archaeological data from modern theological speculation across the silent cities of the Indus.

Dispatch 01922 Nov 202613 min read

Walk into any major Bronze Age ruin outside South Asia—Karnak in Egypt or Ur in Mesopotamia—and the architecture screams power. Soaring stone pillars, colossal statues of pharaohs, and ziggurats built for city gods intimidate the visitor. Then walk through Mohenjo-daro or Harappa. You find wide brick avenues, immaculate drains, comfortable multi-room houses, and public bathing tanks, but no towering pyramids, no imperial statues, and not a single identifiable temple.

For a century, archaeologists have wrestled with this silence. When early excavators found clay figurines and horned figures on seals, they immediately projected later religious ideas backward, christening them "Priest-Kings", "Mother Goddesses", and "Proto-Shiva". But does the physical evidence justify these grand labels, or have we merely built temples of our own mind upon their quiet streets?

At a glance

→  No monumental temples, royal tombs, or grandiose palaces exist in the Indus civilisation.

→  Terracotta figurines and seals hint at ritual beliefs, but interpretations remain deeply contested.

→  Archaeological evidence reveals regional ritual variety rather than a uniform Bronze Age state religion.

The larger picture

Context

To understand why Harappan religion is such a fierce battleground, we have to look back to the early days of its discovery in the 1920s[1][8]. When Sir John Marshall announced the civilisation to the world in 1924, South Asian urban history was pushed back over two thousand years into the third millennium BCE[1][9]. Marshall and his colleagues were trained in Western classical archaeology and colonial administrative categories. When they uncovered enigmatic clay figurines, horned male figures seated cross-legged on miniature steatite seals, and sacred pipal leaf motifs, Marshall took a bold interpretative leap in 1931[8]. He argued that Harappan religion represented the direct linear ancestor of modern Hinduism, identifying a Great Male God, a Mother Goddess, and tree-and-animal worship[8].

For decades, Marshall's synthesis went largely unquestioned in textbooks. However, as independent India saw extensive surveys by archaeologists like A. Ghosh and B. B. Lal, the geographic picture shifted drastically[1]. The civilisation was no longer confined to the Indus river valley in modern Pakistan; hundreds of settlements were discovered along the dried Ghaggar-Hakra system and across Gujarat and Rajasthan[1][11]. At sites like Kalibangan, excavated by B. B. Lal in 1961, archaeologists did not find the female clay figurines so common in Mohenjo-daro, but rather baked-brick pits identified as fire altars[11].

Modern historians such as Upinder Singh emphasise that because the Indus script remains undeciphered, every claim about Harappan religion, governance, and social hierarchy remains provisional[9][10]. Without written hymns, liturgical manuals, or royal declarations, material objects can be read in many conflicting ways[9]. Today, scholars like Jonathan Mark Kenoyer insist on treating the Indus cities on their own terms, examining the concrete distribution of artefacts rather than imposing modern or historical theological frameworks onto silent Bronze Age stones[3].

Today's lesson

The Ghost of the Priest-King: Power Without Palaces

In 1927, excavators at Mohenjo-daro pulled a miniature steatite bust out of the brick rubble. Just under eighteen centimetres tall, the bearded figure wore an armband, a fillet around his neatly combed hair, and a robe draped over his left shoulder decorated with trefoil patterns. John Marshall immediately identified this sculpture as a 'Priest-King'[8]. That single phrase ignited nearly a century of popular myth-making. Mesopotamian cities had their ensi—kings who also served as high priests—and Bronze Age Egypt had divine pharaohs who commanded armies and built pyramids. Colonial archaeologists, expecting ancient civilisations to conform to an identical blueprint of absolute hierarchy, assumed the Indus Valley must also possess an imperial ruling caste that blended religious autocracy with civil governance[7][8].

Yet, across thousands of square miles of Harappan territory, no one has ever uncovered a royal tomb, a palace, or a grand monumental temple[7][8]. There are no colossal victory stelae celebrating a conquering general, no carved murals depicting chained war captives, and no evidence of a standing royal army[7][9]. The famous steatite bust remains virtually unique. As modern scholars like Upinder Singh point out, applying the grandiose title of 'Priest-King' to an isolated statuette says far more about nineteenth-century European assumptions than it does about Harappan reality[2][8].

Instead of autocratic monarchs directing resources toward personal mausoleums, Harappan cities invested their collective wealth in public infrastructure: brick drainage networks, paved roads, and civic water management[7]. Historians increasingly lean toward models of civic councils, merchant guilds, or regional federations rather than an all-powerful royal court[3][7]. If political authority existed, it appears to have been shared among regional elites, civic administrators, and kinship clans[7][8]. The dramatic absence of dynastic monuments forces us to abandon our old assumptions about ancient political violence and absolute theocracy[6][7]. The Harappans built an enduring civilisation where order was maintained through municipal coordination, shared economic standards, and civic discipline rather than royal terror[3][7].

The Mother Goddess Debate: Domestic Clay and Selective Survival

Visit any museum displaying Harappan antiquities, and you will almost certainly encounter miniature earthenware figurines of women. Hand-modelled with pinched noses, elaborate fan-shaped headdresses, heavy pellet necklaces, and short skirts, these figures captivated John Marshall and his team during the 1920s[8]. Marshall immediately classified them as representations of a pan-Indus 'Mother Goddess', drawing a direct lineage to fertility cults in the ancient Near East and later village goddess worship across rural India[8]. For generations of school textbooks, the existence of a pervasive Harappan goddess cult was repeated as an incontestable historical truth.

However, modern archaeological surveys tell a much more complicated and fragmented story[9]. As historian Upinder Singh emphasises, the distribution of these female figurines is strikingly uneven across the subcontinent[9]. They appear in great abundance in the western metropolises of Mohenjo-daro and Harappa, but they are almost completely absent from major contemporary sites in Gujarat and Rajasthan, such as Lothal and Kalibangan[9][11]. If this figurine cult was truly the official state religion of the Indus civilisation, why did eastern and southern Harappan cities neglect it entirely?

Furthermore, interpreting the function of clay objects without deciphered texts is notoriously fraught[9]. Anthropologists and archaeologists note that small terracotta human and animal figurines can serve a bewildering variety of everyday purposes[9]. They could be protective talismans for childbed illnesses, ancestral votive offerings, domestic representations of clan lineage, or simply children's toys[9]. In rural societies throughout history, people shaped clay into miniature forms for domestic rituals that had nothing to do with state theology or formal priesthoods[8]. By automatically elevating every terracotta female figurine into an all-powerful fertility goddess, earlier archaeologists mistook what was likely a localised, domestic folk tradition for a universal Bronze Age state church[8][9].

Pashupati and the Horned Mask: Reading Ritual Iconography

Perhaps the most famous piece of religious evidence from Mohenjo-daro is a small steatite stamp seal depicting a horned figure seated cross-legged on a low dais. Flanked by an elephant, a tiger, a rhinoceros, and a water buffalo, with two deer beneath the seat, the figure wears an elaborate headdress topped with horns. In 1931, John Marshall famously christened this image 'Proto-Shiva'[8]. He argued that the figure sat in a classic yogic position, exhibited three faces, and represented the mythological archetype of Shiva as Pashupati, the lord of the beasts, and Mahayogi, the great ascetic[8]. This visual comparison became an established cornerstone of Indian historiography.

Yet, critical scholars have repeatedly challenged this retrospective interpretation[8]. Historians like Wendy Doniger have pointed out that projecting classical Puranic traditions backwards across two thousand years into the third millennium BCE is an act of interpretative imagination rather than objective analysis[8]. Close physical examination of the seal reveals details that resist easy theological pigeonholing. Archaeologists still debate whether the figure is male, female, or supernatural; whether it has three faces or merely a stylised bovine profile; and whether the posture is genuinely yogic or simply a comfortable seated position common across ancient Asia[8].

Terracotta horned and bearded masks discovered across Indus sites indicate that horned headgear was an important ritual or elite signifier, but this does not automatically prove a connection to Shiva[8]. The horned motif appears widely across prehistoric Eurasia as an emblem of shamanic power, hunting magic, or clan hierarchy. As Jonathan Mark Kenoyer and Upinder Singh have noted, Harappan iconography emerged from indigenous South Asian traditions that interacted with neighbouring cultures, such as those in Central Asia and Baluchistan[3][8]. To reduce these rich, enigmatic visual symbols to convenient antecedents of later classical deities is to erase the unique, lost mental universe of the Bronze Age people who actually carved them[8][9].

Civic Waters and Brick Altars: Regional Diversity over State Orthodoxy

If monumental state temples did not exist, where did Harappan religious life actually happen? The archaeological evidence points to two distinct spaces: the domestic home and regional civic structures[8][9]. Consider Mohenjo-daro's famous Great Bath, a masterwork of brick architecture lined with bitumen waterproofing. Scholars widely agree that this monumental tank was built for public, ritual purification rather than mere recreation[8]. In South Asian culture, ritual bathing has deep historical resonances, and it is tempting to see the Great Bath as the ancestral prototype for later temple tanks. Yet, remarkably, the Great Bath is unique to Mohenjo-daro; no equivalent structure has ever been found at Harappa, Dholavira, or Rakhigarhi[3][8].

When we turn our attention east to Rajasthan, an entirely different ritual landscape emerges[1][11]. At Kalibangan, excavated by renowned Indian archaeologist B. B. Lal in 1961, archaeologists found no Great Bath and almost no female clay figurines[11]. Instead, Lal uncovered a row of seven baked-brick pits on a civic platform, containing charcoal, ash, and cylindrical clay bricks, which he interpreted as fire altars used for sacrificial rites[11]. Similar hearth structures were later reported at Lothal in Gujarat[11]. Lal argued that these altars demonstrated that fire-centric worship, reminiscent of Vedic rituals, was already practised in the mature Harappan period[1][11].

However, other archaeologists dispute Lal's interpretation, suggesting these hearths may have served practical domestic, culinary, or craft functions rather than Vedic sacrificial rituals. What the archaeological record unequivocally proves is that the Harappan world was not a rigid, ideologically uniform empire[3][9]. As Jonathan Mark Kenoyer has argued, Harappan urbanism was a vast cultural mosaic shaped by long-term regional adaptations[3]. From the water sanctums of the lower Indus to the brick fire hearths of Rajasthan and the domestic courtyards of Punjab, spiritual life across the civilisation was decentralised, regionally diverse, and tied closely to civic daily living[3][8][11].

How do we know? · the historian's workshop

Can We Identify a Unified 'Harappan Religion' Without Deciphered Texts?

The evidence, step by step

1.
Artefacts such as female terracotta figurines are dense at Mohenjo-daro and Harappa but virtually absent in Rajasthan and Gujarat[9][11].
2.
Distinctive ritual structures vary sharply by region, from the Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro to brick-lined fire altars at Kalibangan[8][11].
3.
No monumental temples, state sanctuaries, or unambiguous ideological inscriptions exist at any excavated site[7][8][9].
∴
Harappan ritual practices were diverse, regionalised, and largely domestic, making claims of a single, unified Bronze Age state religion historically unsupportable[3][8][9].

Archaeology relies on material proxies to infer cognitive, social, and spiritual worlds[3]. When dealing with an undeciphered culture, the risk of confirmation bias is extraordinarily high[9]. Sir John Marshall and earlier scholars operated by projection: they took symbolic forms from historical Hinduism—such as yogic postures, sacred trees, phallic stones, and female deities—and read them backwards across a two-thousand-year gap[8].

However, the material record reveals deep regional variation rather than ideological uniformity[3][9]. The Great Bath stands alone at Mohenjo-daro; the fire altars belong to Kalibangan and Lothal; female terracotta figurines are concentrated in the western heartland[8][11]. Without textual verification, assigning a theological identity to these disparate physical remains pushes past archaeology into creative speculation[8][9].

Close reading

“Archaeological evidence reveals a great deal about this civilization—its varied subsistence base, vibrant craft traditions, and extensive trade networks—but given the non-decipherment of the script, conclusions about many other aspects such as religion, society, and polity remain speculative.”

Upinder Singh, A History of Ancient and Early Medieval India, 2008, p. 181[9]

In this assessment, historian Upinder Singh establishes a sharp epistemological boundary for Indus studies[9]. While material culture provides rock-solid data about technological capabilities—such as the standardisation of chert weights, the pyrotechnology of brick baking, and international bead commerce—it remains silent on internal cognitive systems[3][9].

Singh warns that without deciphered writing, modern observers routinely superimpose their own cultural anxieties or retrospective religious nationalist narratives onto mute artefacts[2][9]. A terracotta female figurine could be an all-powerful fertility goddess, a votive token offered for healing, an ancestral spirit, or merely a child's toy[9]. By insisting on the label 'speculative', Singh reminds us that intellectual honesty in ancient history requires knowing where hard evidence ends and interpretative conjecture begins[9].

How historians read it

Scholarly interpretation of Harappan spiritual life has evolved across three major phases. In the colonial era, Sir John Marshall established the 'Proto-Hindu' framework, interpreting seals, bath architecture, and figurines through the lens of later classical traditions[8]. By the late twentieth century, Indian archaeologists like B. B. Lal drew attention to the fire altars at Kalibangan, arguing for tangible ritual continuities with later Vedic traditions[1][11].

Conversely, modern revisionists, including Jonathan Mark Kenoyer and Upinder Singh, take a more cautious, contextual approach[2][3][9]. They stress that Harappan urbanism emerged from regional indigenous developments, displaying localised ritual expressions rather than an all-encompassing state orthodoxy[3][9]. Meanwhile, scholars like Wendy Doniger warn sharply against retrofitting later complex mythologies onto ambiguous Bronze Age emblems[8].

The strongest objection

Scholarly proponents of continuity contend that the material parallels between Harappan ritual items and historical Indian religious practices are simply too consistent to be dismissed as mere coincidence[1][11]. The presence of dedicated brick fire altars containing ash and charcoal at Kalibangan, ritual bathing installations like Mohenjo-daro's Great Bath, the veneration of the pipal tree on steatite seals, and figures seated in cross-legged yogic postures provide tangible material evidence of an unbroken cultural and ritual continuity linking the Bronze Age directly to later indigenous traditions[8][11].

The best reply

Upinder Singh and contemporary critical archaeologists reply that formal visual resemblance does not prove identical meaning or uninterrupted religious lineage[8][9]. Many agricultural societies venerate trees, practice water purification, or build hearths without sharing a common theology[8]. Furthermore, crucial elements of later traditions are completely absent from Harappan sites, while the uneven geographical spread of figurines and altars suggests localised practices rather than a single ancestral faith[9][11]. Without deciphered inscriptions, claiming direct continuity remains an attractive hypothesis rather than an established archaeological fact[8][9].

Fact, interpretation & debate

Archaeological evidence firmly establishes that the Harappan civilisation lacked monumental temples, royal mausoleums, or grandiose imperial palaces[7][8]. Public infrastructure like Mohenjo-daro's Great Bath and standardised civic layouts are verified material facts[3][8]. However, religious interpretations are intensely contested. While Sir John Marshall famously proposed that female clay figurines and the horned seal represented a 'Mother Goddess' and 'Proto-Shiva'[8], modern historians like Upinder Singh caution that retrofitting later classical Hindu concepts onto undeciphered Bronze Age artefacts is highly speculative[9]. Archaeologist B. B. Lal highlighted brick hearths at Kalibangan as fire altars[11], but scholars like Jonathan Mark Kenoyer stress that without readable scripts, distinguishing between everyday domestic hearths and sacred liturgy remains guesswork[3][9].

Why it matters now

Contemporary connections

How we interpret the silent ruins of the Indus Valley directly shapes modern debates on history, identity, and politics in South Asia. In digital forums and public discourse across India, people frequently debate whether Harappan culture represents an ancestral Vedic civilisation or an entirely distinct prehistoric urban tradition[3][4]. When early colonial scholars labelled clay artefacts as 'Priest-Kings' or 'Mother Goddesses', they established visual narratives that modern readers still absorb in school textbooks[8][9]. Recognizing the limits of archaeological evidence teaches us how easily historical interpretations can be driven by modern nationalist or ideological desires rather than objective physical data[9].

Beyond political debates, the Harappan model offers a provocative mirror for modern urban planning. In an era dominated by vanity architecture—colossal skyscrapers, private estate mega-projects, and grand political monuments—the Harappans chose a radically different path. They invested their collective economic surplus in underground sewer networks, public water filtration, uniform weights, and standardized housing[1][3][7]. Their civic life reminds contemporary planners in rapidly expanding Indian cities like Bengaluru or NCR that a society's true sophistication lies in egalitarian public infrastructure and municipal hygiene, not in extravagant monuments built for political elites[7].

In practice

Practical applications

Studying how historians evaluate Harappan religion provides a powerful mental tool for everyday analytical thinking: separating raw data from retrospective narrative projection. Whenever you encounter arguments in corporate strategy, news media, or political debates, learn to distinguish between verifiable material facts and the stories people wrap around them. Just as archaeologists caution against reading modern theological concepts back into unlettered 4,000-year-old artefacts[8][9], critical thinkers must avoid confirmation bias—the tendency to fit ambiguous evidence into familiar existing mental categories. Applying this rigorous empirical standard helps you evaluate claims objectively, whether analyzing financial data, reading headline news, or assessing corporate leadership.

In one breath

The Indus civilisation developed an astonishing urban order without the grandiose temples, royal mausoleums, or imperial monuments seen in Bronze Age Egypt and Mesopotamia[7][8]. Because their script remains undeciphered, identifying famous seals and terracotta figurines as ancestral Hindu deities remains an interpretative speculation rather than established fact[8][9]. Instead, the archaeological evidence points to a decentralised society marked by regional ritual diversity, domestic ceremonies, and civic municipal coordination[3][7][9].

The silence of the Indus cities is not a void to be filled with modern assumptions, but a reminder of historical humility. When we stop demanding that ancient people fit our modern categories, their real achievement—a peaceful, orderly, and hygienic urban world—shines through clearly.
