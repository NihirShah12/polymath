The Slave Who Rebuilt Freedom: Epictetus and Stoic Inner Sovereignty

How an enslaved Greek in Nero's Rome transformed personal subjection into the ancient world's most radical philosophy of inner freedom.

Dispatch 01013 Nov 202627 min read

Imagine walking into a lecture hall in Nicopolis, a windy coastal city in northwestern Greece, around the year 108 CE[1]. The man standing before you—or rather, sitting heavily, leaning on a wooden stick—is an old freedman with a pronounced limp[1][8]. He has no formal family name of his own; the word by which history knows him, Epictetus, is simply the Greek participle for 'acquired'[3][6]. He was born into slavery in Hierapolis, at the eastern fringes of the Roman Empire, and served in the treacherous household of Nero’s administrative secretary in Rome[1][9]. Yet before him sit sons of Roman senators, future provincial governors, and wealthy aristocrats, all hanging on his every word[4][11].

How did a former piece of human chattel become the most revered ethical authority of the Mediterranean world, outstripping even Plato in popular renown among later readers[4]? The paradox of Epictetus lies in his radical redefinition of human liberty. In a society where a master could break a slave's leg with impunity[8], Epictetus declared that no tyrant, no leg-iron, and no executioner could ever enslave a human mind that understood its own power[3][10]. Today, we begin our four-day journey into his life and thought, tracing how an unwritten teaching preserved by an attentive student became a cornerstone of Western philosophy[3][8].

The larger picture

Context

To understand the world that forged Epictetus, one must look at the Roman Empire during the first century of the Christian era—a period defined by unprecedented material luxury, absolute monarchical power, and political terror. The empire was not a serene republic of deliberative citizens; it was an autocracy where the whim of an emperor like Nero or Domitian could mean instant exile, torture, or death for even the highest-ranking senators[1][3][12].

For the traditional Roman elite, who had long measured their personal worth by political office, military command, and public honor, this despotic political reality induced a profound existential crisis. When civic institutions no longer afforded security or meaningful self-determination, where was a human being to find dignity? It was precisely in response to this political helplessness that Stoicism transformed itself from an abstract Greek philosophical system into an urgent, practical art of survival and moral resistance[4][7].

Stoicism had originally been founded in Athens around 300 BCE by Zeno of Citium, who taught under the Painted Porch (Stoa Poikile)[4]. Early Stoicism encompassed three interconnected disciplines: logic, physics (the study of nature and cosmology), and ethics[4]. But by the time Stoicism reached Imperial Rome, its center of gravity had shifted dramatically toward ethics[4]. Philosophers were no longer merely lecturing on cosmological principles; they were serving as spiritual directors to individuals navigating the perilous corridors of imperial power or the harsh realities of subjection[4][7].

Into this volatile environment Epictetus was born, around 50–55 CE, in Hierapolis, a Greek city in Phrygia (modern-day Pamukkale in Turkey)[1][3]. As a boy, he was brought to Rome as the slave of Epaphroditus, a powerful freedman who served as a high-ranking administrative officer in Nero’s imperial court[1][9]. Living in the household of Epaphroditus placed the young slave at the very epicenter of imperial intrigue and brutality[1]. Yet, remarkably, his owner permitted him to attend the lectures of Musonius Rufus, the premier Stoic teacher of the age[1][3][11].

Musonius Rufus was a Roman knight of unbending moral severity[1][12]. He did not offer smooth rhetoric to soothe elite sensibilities; he insisted that philosophy was an uncompromising training regimen for the soul[7][12]. Musonius paid dearly for his intellectual integrity, being exiled three times by successive emperors—including Nero, who banished him to the barren, rocky island of Gyaros[12]. For the young Epictetus, Musonius Rufus provided a living model of Stoic resilience: a man whose inner tranquility remained untouched whether he was addressing eager pupils in Rome or enduring hardship on a desolate rock in the Aegean[12].

Sometime after the death of Nero in 68 CE, Epictetus received his manumission—his formal emancipation from slavery[8]. Now a freedman, he began to teach philosophy in Rome[7][8]. But his public career in the imperial capital was cut short around 89–93 CE, when the emperor Domitian issued a decree expelling all philosophers from Rome and Italy[3][4][8]. Domitian viewed Stoic philosophers with intense suspicion, irritated by their popularity among senatorial dissidents who opposed his autocratic rule[3][11].

Rather than abandoning his vocation, Epictetus joined the philosophical exodus and traveled to Nicopolis, a Greek city in Epirus situated on the northwestern coast of Greece[3][4]. There, far from the toxic politics of the Roman court, he established his own philosophical school[4][8]. It was in Nicopolis that Epictetus spent the remaining decades of his life, attracting students from across the Mediterranean, living in radical simplicity, and leaving an indelible mark on ancient thought through his fiery oral lectures, recorded by his pupil Arrian[1][4][10].

Today's lesson

The Household of Epaphroditus: Living in the Shadow of the Throne

To appreciate the force of Epictetus's philosophy, one must first step inside the Roman household where he spent his youth as a slave[1][9]. His master, Epaphroditus, was a powerful freedman who had risen through the imperial administrative bureaucracy to become a senior personal secretary to Emperor Nero[1][9]. In the ancient Roman world, the household of an imperial administrator was not a peaceful domestic sanctuary; it was a high-stakes pressure cooker of political ambition, court paranoia, and sudden violence[1]. Servants and slaves in such households observed firsthand how quickly an imperial favorite could fall from grace, how massive wealth and high social rank offered zero guarantee against execution, and how men who commanded legions lived in perpetual terror of a monarch’s unpredictable mood[1].

Epictetus was given no proper personal name at birth; he was known simply as Epiktētos, a generic Greek descriptor meaning 'acquired' or 'bought'[3][6]. Born around 50–55 CE in Hierapolis, a Greek city in Phrygia (located in present-day Turkey), he entered the world as legal chattel (res)[1][3]. Yet, in a testament to the complex social dynamics of Roman slavery, Epaphroditus permitted the young slave to attend the lectures of the Stoic master Lucius Annaeus Musonius Rufus[1][3][11]. It was not entirely uncommon for wealthy Roman masters to permit intellectually gifted slaves to receive advanced education, often to enhance the household's prestige or administrative utility[11]. But for Epictetus, this permission proved fundamentally transformative[8][9].

In the lecture room of Musonius, Epictetus encountered an intellectual framework that completely inverted the social power structure of the Roman Empire[1][11]. While Roman jurisprudence defined Epictetus as an object owned by Epaphroditus, Stoic philosophy asserted that the vast majority of 'free' Romans were, in reality, total slaves—slaves to their passions, their anxieties, their thirst for status, and their terror of pain and death[3][7]. Conversely, a person in physical bonds who possessed full governance over their internal judgements was truly free[3][10].

This stark contrast was not a theoretical exercise for Epictetus; it was demonstrated daily in the imperial court[1]. Epictetus watched powerful administrative officials tremble before Nero while forced to feign adulation[1]. He saw that legal liberty and political power did not confer peace of mind[3][10]. The true division in human existence was not between slave and free citizen, but between the uneducated mind that blamed external circumstances for its distress and the educated mind that recognized its own internal sovereignty[3][7]. This early exposure to supreme imperial authority, observed from the absolute bottom of the social hierarchy, permanently forged Epictetus's teaching voice: direct, unvarnished, satirical, and fiercely urgent[1][10].

The Pedagogy of Musonius Rufus: Iron, Philosophy, and Action

The decisive intellectual encounter of Epictetus’s youth was his apprenticeship under Lucius Annaeus Musonius Rufus, the preeminent Stoic teacher of first-century Rome[1][3]. Musonius was a Roman knight of unyielding moral gravity whose uncompromising principles repeatedly brought him into direct collision with imperial authority[1][12]. Musonius firmly believed that philosophy was not an academic specialization, a rhetorical game, or a decorative pastime for wealthy aristocrats; it was an urgent, daily discipline of soul-craft akin to medical treatment or athletic conditioning[7][12].

Musonius’s classroom methodology was direct and deliberately unsparing[1]. He famously remarked to his students that if a listener had leisure to applaud a philosopher's lecture, the teacher had failed; the proper response to a genuine philosophical lecture was not applause, but a silent, searching sorrow for one's own moral deficiencies[1]. He demanded that his pupils test their doctrines in the practical arena of daily conduct, advocating simple food, modest clothing, manual labor, and equal philosophical education for women[12].

For Epictetus, Musonius was far more than an instructor; he was the living archetype of Stoic fortitude[1][12]. Musonius was banished three times during his life by Roman emperors[12]. Under Nero, he was exiled to Gyaros, a barren, water-scarce island in the northern Cyclades used as a high-security penal colony for political enemies[12]. Where others saw devastation and exile, Musonius treated Gyaros as a neutral external condition—an opportunity to practice virtue and cultivate inner tranquility without the corrupting distractions of urban luxury[12]. Later, under Vespasian, he was exiled again to Syria, yet he returned to Rome each time with his moral purpose completely intact[12].

Epictetus frequently cited Musonius in his own later lectures at Nicopolis, recalling how his master tested his students' intellectual and moral stamina[1][11]. In one characteristic anecdote preserved in the Discourses, Epictetus remembered Musonius pressing a student with difficult logical syllogisms[1]. When the student complained that he had not committed a major crime like burning down the Roman Capitol, Musonius sharply replied that in logic, as in life, committing an error in judgement is itself the capitol crime[1][10]. From Musonius Rufus, Epictetus learned that the ultimate purpose of logic was to guard moral volition (prohairesis) against self-deception and emotional passion[1][3]. He absorbed his master’s conviction that a teacher must speak directly to the student’s conscience, stripping away vanity with iron moral clarity[1][11].

The Lame Philosopher: Sickness, Injury, and the Unconquered Will

Throughout his adult life, Epictetus was physically disabled, walking with a severe limp and enduring frail health[1][3][8]. Ancient historical sources present divergent accounts regarding how his physical impairment originated[8]. Celsus, as quoted by the Christian scholar Origen in Contra Celsum, recorded a famous account that Epictetus’s master, Epaphroditus, had deliberately tortured his slave by twisting his leg in a rack[8]. According to this narrative, Epictetus calmly warned his master, 'You are going to break it,' and when the bone snapped, added without a trace of anger, 'Did I not tell you that you would break it?'[8].

Conversely, the later Neoplatonic commentator Simplicius made no mention of deliberate master cruelty, writing simply that Epictetus had suffered from severe lameness and physical illness from early childhood[8]. Modern scholars generally regard the story of deliberate leg-breaking as potentially moralized legend, while emphasizing the indisputable historical reality of his lifelong physical disability[1][8]. Regardless of its precise historical origin, Epictetus's lameness occupied a central place in his philosophical identity and his radical teaching on the human body[1][3].

In Stoic physical theory, the human body is classified as an 'indifferent' (adiaphoron)—a physical condition that may be preferred (health) or dispreferred (illness), but which never constitutes a moral good or evil in itself[3][7]. Your body, Epictetus repeatedly hammered home to his students, does not belong to you; it is merely refined clay, a fragile vessel borrowed temporarily from universal nature[3][10]. External forces—disease, physical accident, old age, or an imperial executioner—can chain, mutilate, or destroy the body at any moment[3][7]. But those external forces possess zero power to penetrate the inner citadel of your moral purpose unless you voluntarily surrender it[3][10].

In the Discourses, Epictetus routinely drew upon his own physical condition to illustrate this doctrine[1][10]. 'Sickness is an impediment to the body, but not to the will (prohairesis), unless the will itself chooses to be impeded,' he declared[1][3]. 'Lameness is an impediment to the leg, but not to the will'[3]. By refusing to allow his physical disability to define his self-worth, peace of mind, or moral efficacy, Epictetus embodied the core Stoic insight that human suffering stems not from physical facts, but from our internal interpretations of those facts[3][7]. His lameness was transformed from a physical liability into a living proof of mental freedom[3][10].

The Expulsion under Domitian: Tyranny and Philosophical Exile

Sometime after the death of Nero in 68 CE, Epictetus received his manumission—his formal legal emancipation from slavery[8]. Now a freedman, he established himself as a teacher of Stoic philosophy in Rome, gaining a rising reputation among students and intellectuals[7][8]. However, by the late 80s CE, political storm clouds were gathering over the imperial capital under the reign of Emperor Domitian, who ruled from 81 to 96 CE[3][8]. Domitian’s rule degenerated into acute autocracy, marked by deep paranoia, widespread political executions, and aggressive treason prosecutions against senators[3][11].

Standing in principled opposition to Domitian’s despotism was an informal network of high-minded senators and intellectuals known to historians as the 'Stoic Opposition.' These men drew direct inspiration from Stoic doctrines to resist imperial tyranny, refusing to flatter the monarch or participate in corrupt political show trials[3][11]. Although Stoicism was not a revolutionary political party, its fundamental insistence that truth, justice, and personal integrity outweighed imperial favor made its practitioners inherent threats to an autocratic regime[3][7].

Irritated by the respect accorded to Stoic critics of his reign, Domitian decided to eliminate the philosophical movement from the capital[3]. Around 89 to 93 CE, he issued an imperial decree expelling all philosophers from Rome and Italy under pain of severe punishment[3][4][8]. Philosophers were driven out of the capital in a state-sanctioned purge intended to silence moral criticism[4][11].

For traditional Roman citizens, banishment from Rome was regarded as a devastating punishment, second only to execution[12]. Banishment meant severance from civic life, loss of social status, and isolation in distant imperial provinces[12]. But for Epictetus, who had absorbed the hard lessons of Musonius Rufus, exile was merely an external event beyond his control, and therefore a matter of complete moral indifference[1][3][12].

Instead of descending into despair or petitioning the imperial court for clemency, Epictetus accepted the banishment calmly[3][4]. He crossed the Ionian Sea to northwestern Greece and selected the colonial city of Nicopolis in Epirus as his new residence[3][4]. What Domitian designed as a punitive suppression of Stoic philosophy resulted instead in the establishment of one of the greatest pedagogical centers of the ancient world[4][8]. Tyranny could expel philosophers from Rome, but it could not exile Stoic truth from the human mind[3][11].

The Nicopolis Seminar: Pedagogy for Future Rulers

Nicopolis, where Epictetus founded his new school around 93 CE, was a vibrant Roman colony established by Emperor Augustus to commemorate his naval victory over Mark Antony and Cleopatra at Actium[3][4][8]. Located near key maritime and overland trade routes in Epirus, Nicopolis was easily reachable by travelers from both Italy and Greece[4]. Situated far from the treacherous intrigues of the Roman court, Epictetus built a thriving academy that quickly earned an international reputation across the Mediterranean world[4][5].

The student body at Nicopolis consisted largely of young men from upper-class Roman and Greek families—the sons of senators, provincial governors, and wealthy equestrian families[4]. These young men were preparing for careers in imperial governance, expecting to serve as military officers, high magistrates, and provincial administrators[4]. They came to Nicopolis to complete their higher education before entering public life[4].

Yet Epictetus did not offer them smooth political coaching, legal training, or ornamental rhetoric[4][7]. Instead, he subjected them to a rigorous moral diagnostic[1][7]. In his classroom, aristocratic lineage and family wealth possessed zero value[4][10]. Epictetus made it bluntly clear that if a young noble was terrified of losing his fortune, desperate for imperial praise, or easily broken by grief, he remained a slave in the eyes of nature[3][10].

The daily routine of the Nicopolis school involved studying the foundational treatises of early Stoic thinkers—such as Zeno of Citium, Cleanthes, and Chrysippus—which covered logic, physics, and ethics[4]. But Epictetus consistently directed the instruction toward practical moral execution[4][7]. He would interrupt textual analysis to examine real-life scenarios: How should a provincial governor react if insulted by a local official? How should a military commander face physical illness or death? How can an administrator maintain integrity when ordered to perform an unjust act[4][7]?

Epictetus lived his teachings with radical authenticity[7]. His household in Nicopolis was famous for its extreme simplicity[10]. He lived in a modest house with minimal furniture, keeping his door unlocked to all comers[10]. Though he never married, late in life he adopted an abandoned infant who would otherwise have died of exposure, hiring a nurse and raising the child out of pure benevolence[1]. Students who entered his school expecting academic lectures found themselves transformed by a living master of soul-craft[4][7].

Flavius Arrian and the Preservation of the Unwritten Word

Like Socrates before him, Epictetus wrote nothing for publication[3][6]. He treated philosophy as a living, spoken encounter between master and pupil, designed to produce immediate moral transformation rather than written texts[4][7]. Had it not been for one exceptionally dedicated student, the vivid, fiery voice of the lame master of Nicopolis would have vanished completely from history[3][4].

That student was Flavius Arrianus—known to history as Arrian—a young Roman aristocrat from Nicomedia in Bithynia who attended Epictetus’s lectures around 108 CE[1][4]. Arrian was far more than a passive student; he would go on to achieve a distinguished public career as a Roman military commander, governor of Cappadocia under Emperor Hadrian, and one of the ancient world’s finest historians, best remembered for his definitive history of Alexander the Great[4]. But during his young adulthood in Nicopolis, Arrian was above all an attentive recording disciple[4][8].

Recognizing the extraordinary moral energy of Epictetus’s lectures, Arrian sat in the back of the lecture hall taking rapid verbatim transcriptions in ancient Greek shorthand[4][8]. He captured not merely the formal logical propositions, but the living cadence of Epictetus’s speech: his biting satirical humor, his dramatic imagined dialogues with hypothetical opponents, his abrupt rebukes, and his passionate invocations of divine providence[1][3].

Arrian compiled these transcriptions into an eight-volume collection entitled the Discourses (Diatribai), of which four books have survived to the present day[3][8]. Recognizing that busy practitioners needed a portable summary, Arrian later extracted the central practical maxims from the Discourses and arranged them into a compact manual titled the Enchiridion (meaning 'Handbook' or 'That Which Is Kept in the Hand')[3][8][11].

In a prefactory letter introducing the Discourses, Arrian wrote that he had not formally 'composed' these books as literary art, but had simply recorded Epictetus’s exact words so that he might preserve for himself the unadulterated memory of his master’s mind and moral force[8]. Thanks to Arrian’s precise stenography, reading the Discourses today feels remarkably like sitting in the Nicopolis classroom, listening directly to a master teacher diagnose human illusions with surgical precision[1][4][10].

Synthesis: The Stoic Teacher as Physician of the Soul

What made Epictetus’s teaching so uniquely resonant—and why did it echo so powerfully across two millennia, influencing historical figures from Marcus Aurelius and early Christian scholars to modern cognitive psychotherapists[3][4][7]? The answer lies in his radical insistence that philosophy is not a theoretical academic discipline, but a medical intervention for human suffering[4][7].

Epictetus explicitly described his lecture hall not as a theater for intellectual performance or rhetorical display, but as a hospital (iatreion)[1]. 'Men, the philosopher's lecture-room is a hospital,' he famously warned his pupils; 'you ought not to walk out of it in pleasure, but in pain, for you are not well when you enter it'[1]. The core illness from which humanity suffers is not physical disease or material poverty, but false judgement (doxa)—the catastrophic error of mistaking external, uncontrollable events for internal, moral goods[3][7][10].

By systematically re-examining human experience, Epictetus distilled Stoic ethics down to a single, unshakeable foundation: the absolute distinction between what is up to us (eph' hēmin) and what is not up to us (ouk eph' hēmin)[3][10][11]. Up to us are our internal judgements, our desires, our aversions, and our moral choices—the sovereign domain he termed prohairesis[1][3]. Not up to us are our physical bodies, our possessions, our social reputations, our public offices, and how other human beings choose to treat us[3][7][10].

All human anxiety, grief, anger, and psychological bondage stem from a single logical failure: attempting to exercise control over things that belong inherently to nature or other people, while neglecting the one thing that is genuinely our own—our moral volition[3][7][10]. When a person fully internalizes this distinction, outer circumstances lose their power to inflict psychological injury[3][7]. A prison becomes merely a room; exile becomes merely a change of geography; poverty becomes merely an absence of superfluous items[7][12].

As we begin our four-day examination of Epictetus, we leave his biographical journey behind and prepare to analyze the core mechanics of his thought. We carry with us the indelible image of the lame freedman standing in his lecture room at Nicopolis, reminding future rulers of Rome that true empire is not the conquest of nations, but the unassailable governance of one's own mind[1][4][10].

The seminar · a philosophical perspective

The Asymmetry of Sovereign Control and Moral Volition

The argument, step by step

1.
P1: External conditions, physical bodies, public positions, and material possessions are subject to forces beyond the individual's direct control.
2.
P2: Moral volition (prohairesis)—the internal capacity to evaluate impressions and give or withhold assent—is intrinsically immune to external compulsion.
3.
P3: Genuine human freedom consists exclusively in the unhindered exercise of that which is entirely within one's sovereign command.
∴
C: Therefore, human freedom and moral well-being are entirely independent of social status or physical circumstances, residing solely in the self-governance of moral volition.

This argument forms the foundational core of Epictetus’s resystematization of Stoic ethics[1]. Structurally, the argument is deductively valid: if P1, P2, and P3 hold, the conclusion C follows as a logical necessity. However, the philosophical weight of the argument rests almost entirely upon Premise 2 and the rigorous definition of 'freedom' embedded in Premise 3. Premise 1 relies upon common empirical observation: a person can be imprisoned, struck by illness, robbed, or executed regardless of their personal desires[3][7]. These external conditions belong to the domain of what Epictetus terms ouk eph' hēmin (not up to us)[3][10]. Premise 2 introduces Epictetus’s crucial psychological claim: that while an oppressor can lock up a body or confiscate property, no external agent can force a person to hold a specific internal evaluation against their own judgement[1][3][10]. If a tyrant demands under threat of death that you declare a falsehood to be true, the tyrant can compel your vocal cords to speak, but cannot compel your internal assent[3][10]. The capacity of volition (prohairesis) remains absolute in its own sphere[1][3]. The controversy lies in Premise 3. By defining true freedom strictly as the unimpeded operation of prohairesis, Epictetus radically narrows the definition of liberty[1][3]. Critics argue that this definition assumes a sharp dualism between mind and body, treating physical bondage as irrelevant to true human flourishing[8][10]. Yet within Epictetus’s Stoic framework, this asymmetry is precisely what enables an enslaved person or a political exile to maintain complete moral equality with—and psychological superiority over—their oppressors[3][10].

Close reading

“Some things are in our control and others not. Things in our control are opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever are our own actions. Things not in our control are body, property, reputation, command, and, in one word, whatever are not our own actions. The things in our control are by nature free, unrestrained, unhindered; but those not in our control are weak, slavish, restrained, belonging to others.”

Enchiridion, Chapter 1 (tr. Robin Hard)

This famous opening line of the Enchiridion—compiled by Arrian from Epictetus’s classroom teaching—serves as the foundational axiom of Epictetus’s entire philosophical program[3][8][11]. Notice first the immediate binary division: 'Some things are in our control (eph' hēmin) and others not (ouk eph' hēmin)'[3][10]. Epictetus does not offer a spectrum of partial control; he demands an absolute categorical sorting[3][10]. The items placed under our control are entirely psychological operations: opinion (hypolēpsis), pursuit (hormē), desire (orexis), and aversion (ekklisis)[3]. These are grouped together as 'whatever are our own actions'[3]. What unites them is that they originate within the governing faculty of the soul (hēgemonikon) and require our personal assent[1]. Conversely, the list of things not in our control includes 'body, property, reputation, command'[3]. To a modern reader—and certainly to an ancient Roman aristocrat—placing the physical body and public command in the category of things 'belonging to others' seems astonishing[3][4]. Yet Epictetus’s logic is uncompromising: because your body can be infected by illness, crippled by accident, or bound by a master without your consent, it cannot be classified as fundamentally your own[3][8]. The second half of the passage establishes the ontological contrast between these two domains[3]. Things in our control are characterized as 'by nature free, unrestrained, unhindered'[3]. They possess an inherent sovereignty; no tyrant can step inside your mind to physically alter your desire or force your opinion[3][10]. By contrast, external things are labeled 'weak, slavish, restrained, belonging to others'[3]. The pedagogical power of this passage lies in its diagnostic clarity[1][4]. Epictetus asserts that human suffering is entirely the product of a category mistake: treating slavish, external things as though they were free and ours, or treating our internal judgements as though they were helpless victims of circumstance[3][7]. By memorizing and repeatedly applying this diagnostic sorting mechanism in daily life, the practitioner learns to detach peace of mind from external instability[3][11].

In conversation

In developing this rigorous ethics of self-management, Epictetus was conscious of his philosophical ancestry[1][3]. He explicitly bypassed the middle and later Stoic modifications to align himself directly with Socrates and Diogenes the Cynic as historical models of the true sage[3]. From Socrates—particularly as portrayed in Plato’s Apology and Crito—Epictetus took the principle that a good human being cannot be harmed by a worse one, because true harm consists solely in damage to one’s own moral character. From Diogenes, he took the ideal of radical independence (autarkeia), living with zero material encumbrance to demonstrate that freedom requires no physical possessions. In doing so, Epictetus was directly responding to the classical Athenian tradition of Plato and Aristotle. While Aristotle held that human flourishing (eudaimonia) required external goods—such as health, modest wealth, noble birth, and good fortune—Epictetus rejected this as a dangerous concession to tyranny. If flourishing depends on external goods, then the tyrant who controls those goods controls your happiness. By stripping ethics down to pure volition (prohairesis), Epictetus insulated moral life from political despotism[1][3]. Later thinkers wrestled intensely with Epictetus's stance. Marcus Aurelius, the Roman Emperor who studied Epictetus’s Discourses, used these principles in his personal journal (Meditations) to maintain internal integrity while bearing the overwhelming burdens of imperial rule[4][7]. Early Christian writers, such as Origen, praised Epictetus for his austere moral piety and profound sense of divine providence, noting his popularity among common readers[3][4][5]. A striking parallel across philosophical traditions appears in classic Indian thought, specifically in the Bhagavad Gita and the Samkhya-Yoga system. In the Gita (2.47), the doctrine of Nishkama Karma commands the seeker to act with absolute dedication to duty while relinquishing all attachment to the external fruits or outcomes of action (karmanya vadhikaraste ma phaleshu kadachana). Similarly, Samkhya philosophy rests upon viveka-khyati—the sharp discriminative discernment between the pure conscious Self (Purusha) and the changing, external material world (Prakriti). Just as Epictetus demands that we distinguish what is eph' hēmin (internal volition) from ouk eph' hēmin (external body and world), Samkhya and the Gita assert that emotional bondage arises from confusing the true self with external, non-self modifications[3][10].

The strongest objection

The strongest objection to Epictetus’s philosophy comes from the critique of political quietism and social alienation, voiced prominently by Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel in his Phenomenology of Spirit and echoed by modern social critics. Hegel argued that the Stoic concept of freedom is purely abstract, negative, and evasive. By retreating into the 'thought-citadel' of internal volition (prohairesis), the Stoic surrenders the real world of political institutions, social justice, and material conditions to tyrants and oppressors[1][3]. If an enslaved person convinces themselves that physical chains, beatings, and legal subjection are 'indifferent' external matters that cannot harm their true self, Stoicism acts as an ideological anesthetic[3][8]. It encourages victims of systemic injustice to accept their bondage calmly rather than organizing to overthrow unjust social structures[7]. Furthermore, critics argue that Epictetus’s psychological claim is empirically flawed: severe physical torture, starvation, or neurological trauma can break a person's cognitive capacities, proving that the mind is not genuinely independent of the body. Thus, far from offering true liberation, Stoic inner freedom operates as a rationalization for political powerlessness, allowing the sufferer to retreat into subjectivism while leaving the tyrant’s whip completely unchallenged in the material world.

The best reply

The Stoic reply to this critique rests upon a deep misunderstanding of how Epictetus envisioned human social roles (prosōpa). Epictetus never advocated passive withdrawal or apathy toward social duties[1][7]. On the contrary, he repeatedly insisted that human beings are naturally social creatures (politika zōia) bound by familial, civic, and cosmic duties[1][4]. Within Epictetus’s framework, taking action to oppose tyranny, care for one's family, or fulfill a public office is part of one's proper role—provided that one acts out of moral integrity rather than attachment to external outcomes[1][7]. Musonius Rufus, Epictetus’s teacher, actively opposed imperial tyranny and was banished three times as a result, while Epictetus’s student Arrian served as a governor and military commander[4][12]. Stoicism does not demand that one tolerate injustice; it demands that one fight injustice without allowing hatred, anger, or personal despair to destroy one’s internal moral character[3][7]. As to the psychological objection regarding extreme physical torture, Epictetus acknowledged human physical fragility, but argued that as long as rational consciousness remains intact, the capacity to give or withhold moral assent remains present[1][3]. Whether this reply fully succeeds remains a point of debate, but it demonstrates that Stoic volition was designed not as an escape from reality, but as an iron foundation for courageous action[7][11].

If our internal volition is genuinely unassailable, does Stoic philosophy eliminate the moral urgency to dismantle unjust political structures?

c. 50–135 CE. Born into slavery in Hierapolis, studied under Musonius Rufus in Rome, and later founded a highly influential philosophical school in Nicopolis, Greece, after being exiled by Domitian.

Musonius Rufus person
A prominent Roman knight and Stoic teacher of unbending moral severity who was exiled three times by successive emperors and served as Epictetus's primary mentor.

Flavius Arrian person
A student of Epictetus in Nicopolis who took shorthand notes of his lectures, compiling them into the extant 'Discourses' and the portable manual 'Enchiridion'.

Prohairesis concept
Epictetus's central psychological term denoting moral volition, the faculty of choice, and the internal capacity to evaluate impressions, which remains sovereign and unassailable by external forces.

Nicopolis place
A Roman colonial city in northwestern Greece founded by Augustus where Epictetus established his school around 93 CE following his expulsion from Rome.

Epaphroditus person
A wealthy imperial freedman and senior administrative secretary to Emperor Nero who owned Epictetus as a slave in Rome and permitted him to study philosophy.

Domitian's Expulsion event
Around 89–93 CE, the Roman emperor Domitian issued a decree banishing all philosophers from Rome and Italy due to their association with senatorial critics of his autocracy.

Enchiridion work
Literally 'that which is kept in the hand' or 'Handbook', this concise distillation of Epictetus's teachings was compiled by Arrian to serve as a portable guide for daily practice.

 

Fact, interpretation & debate

Historians possess only a skeletal framework for the life of Epictetus, relying on brief, scattered references in classical texts[4]. The primary biographical contours—his birth in Hierapolis around 50–55 CE, his enslavement under Epaphroditus in Rome, his studies with Musonius Rufus, his manumission, his exile under Domitian around 89–93 CE, and his subsequent school in Nicopolis until his death around 135 CE—are well-established through consensus across sources such as the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy and ancient testimonies[1][3][4]. Significant historical and interpretive contests persist regarding the exact origin of Epictetus's lifelong physical disability. Celsus, as quoted by Origen in 'Contra Celsum', claimed that Epictetus's master deliberately broke his leg in a torture rack, whereas the later Neoplatonic commentator Simplicius asserted simply that he had been lame from childhood or early illness[8]. Modern scholars generally view the torture narrative as a moralized hagiographic legend designed to underscore his steadfast endurance, while accepting his physical impairment as an indisputable reality[1][8]. Furthermore, scholars debate the exact degree to which Arrian's written transcriptions (Discourses) preserve Epictetus's exact spoken idiom versus Arrian's own literary structuring, though linguistic analysis confirms a remarkably faithful transmission of his characteristic conversational style[1][8].

Why it matters now

Contemporary connections

The resurgence of Epictetus in contemporary life is most visible in the clinical domain through Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) and Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT). In the mid-20th century, Aaron Beck and Albert Ellis independently recognized that their foundational therapeutic insight—that psychological distress is caused not by external events themselves, but by the cognitive interpretations we attach to them—was a direct descendant of Epictetus's famous maxim in Chapter 5 of the Enchiridion: 'Men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of things.' This ancient diagnostic framework underpins modern clinical interventions used daily by millions worldwide to treat anxiety, depression, and trauma. Beyond psychotherapy, Epictetus has exerted a profound influence on modern leadership under extreme pressure. Most famously, United States Navy Vice Admiral James Stockdale drew directly upon the teachings of Epictetus to survive over seven years of brutal torture and solitary confinement as a prisoner of war in the 'Hanoi Hilton' during the Vietnam War. Stockdale later recounted that realizing he was entering 'the world of Epictetus'—where external honors and physical comfort were stripped away, leaving only his internal moral volition (prohairesis)—enabled him to resist his captors without breaking psychologically. In contemporary India, the echoes of Epictetus resonate strongly within both corporate executive training and indigenous philosophical revivals. Modern Indian boardrooms increasingly turn to Stoic resilience and the dichotomy of control to navigate market volatility, regulatory uncertainty, and high-stakes decision-making. Simultaneously, Indian intellectuals and practitioners often draw striking parallels between Epictetus's prohairesis and traditional Vedic psychological frameworks such as Viveka (discriminative discernment) found in Samkhya and the detached action (Nishkama Karma) of the Bhagavad Gita. Across these diverse contemporary spheres—from clinical psychology to military survival and corporate leadership—Epictetus remains a vital touchstone for individuals seeking inner sovereignty in an unpredictable world[3][7].

In practice

Practical applications

The primary practical tool derived from Epictetus is the daily practice of the 'Dichotomy of Control.' Upon waking and whenever anxiety or frustration arises during the day, pause and subject every troubling event to a strict diagnostic filter: Is this matter entirely within my direct control (my judgements, desires, and actions), or does it belong to the external world (the opinions of others, economic conditions, physical health, or future outcomes)? When faced with a professional setback, public criticism, or interpersonal conflict, immediately disengage emotional energy from the external outcome—which you cannot command—and redirect your focus toward your own immediate response and moral integrity. By consciously training your mind to treat external obstacles as neutral raw material for virtue rather than as catastrophes, you eliminate the exhausting cycle of reactive anger and helplessness. Keep a portable mental ledger tracking moments when you surrendered your inner peace to things outside your control, steadily strengthening your internal sovereign faculty (prohairesis) through daily habituation.

Words worth owning

Prohairesis — Epictetus's central philosophical term denoting moral volition, the faculty of choice, and the rational capacity to evaluate impressions and give or withhold assent.
“For Epictetus, true human freedom is found exclusively in the unhindered exercise of one's prohairesis.”

Adiaphora — Things indifferent; external circumstances such as health, wealth, social status, and physical body that possess no inherent moral good or evil.
“In Stoic ethics, physical illness and poverty are classified as adiaphora because they do not compromise the virtue of the soul.”

Iatreion — Literally a hospital or medical clinic; Epictetus's description of the philosophical lecture room as a place for healing false judgements rather than displaying rhetorical skill.
“When students entered his school expecting academic entertainment, Epictetus reminded them that his classroom was an iatreion for ailing souls.”

When an emperor's decree can banish you from your home, a master can chain your limbs, and illness can weaken your frame, what remains truly your own? Epictetus answers with uncompromising clarity: the citadel of your moral choice. The measure of a human life is not what happens to the body, but how the soul governs itself in the storm.
