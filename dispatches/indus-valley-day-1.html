How Three Archaeologists Pushed Indian History Back Two Millennia

In 1924, three excavators linked two distant mounds and rewrote the antiquity of South Asia.

Dispatch 01417 Nov 202612 min read

Until the early 1920s, history textbooks across the world taught a tidy, settled story. Civilisation had dawned along the Nile and the Euphrates, while Indian history began much later, roughly around the first millennium BCE with Vedic hymns and the rise of northern cities. If you asked an archaeologist in 1900 where India's earliest brick foundations lay, they would point you to Buddhist stupas or the capitals of the Mauryan Empire[7][10].

Then came a stray square seal carved from soft stone, stamped with an animal no living farmer had ever seen. In the space of barely four seasons of digging, three men—two Indian field archaeologists and an English administrator—pulled back the curtain on a vast, interconnected urban world. At a single stroke, they pushed the documented antiquity of Indian civilisation back by two thousand years[9][12].

At a glance

→  Indian history was once believed to begin with Vedic culture around 1500 BCE.

→  Excavators at Harappa and Mohenjo-daro uncovered identical Bronze Age cities 600 kilometres apart.

The larger picture

Context

To understand why this discovery sent shockwaves through global archaeology, one must look at how British colonial scholarship understood ancient India. Throughout the nineteenth century, colonial administrators assumed that urbanism in South Asia was a late import. They saw Indian cities as secondary phenomena that flourished only after contact with Persian empires or Alexander the Great's campaign in 326 BCE.

Scattered clues had appeared earlier, but nobody understood their meaning. In the mid-nineteenth century, British engineers laying ballast for the Lahore-Multan railway line had systematically quarried millions of high-quality fired bricks from a massive mound named Harappa in western Punjab[1]. They treated the ruins as an inexhaustible, free brick quarry, completely unaware that they were grinding down five-thousand-year-old structures[1]. When Alexander Cunningham, the founder of the Archaeological Survey of India (ASI), visited Harappa in the 1850s and 1870s, he collected curious steatite seals bearing an undeciphered script, but he guessed they were foreign or perhaps early historic curiosities.

The institutional turning point arrived under Lord Curzon, who passed the Ancient Monuments Preservation Act in 1904 and appointed a young classical archaeologist named John Marshall as Director-General of the ASI[7][11]. Marshall had dug at Knossos in Crete under Arthur Evans, where excavators were unearthing the Bronze Age Minoan civilisation[7]. He arrived in India determined to build a professional survey and train local talent. Over the next two decades, Marshall reorganised Indian archaeology, fought for survey budgets, and placed gifted Indian officers in charge of regional circles[7][8]. What he could not anticipate was that two of these officers, digging at two remote mounds hundreds of kilometres apart, were about to collide with the same forgotten world.

Today's lesson

The Brick Quarry at Harappa

The road to the discovery began in western Punjab along the dried-out old bed of the Ravi River[1]. By 1920, Harappa was already famous among archaeologists for all the wrong reasons. The site had been stripped of its uppermost architectural layers by railway contractors who carted away thousands of wagon-loads of prehistoric baked brick[1]. What remained looked like a chaotic landscape of scarred earthen mounds.

John Marshall directed Rai Bahadur Daya Ram Sahni, a brilliant Punjabi scholar trained in Sanskrit and archaeology, to begin systematic test excavations[1][4][7]. Sahni, who hailed from the town of Bhera, was known for his rigorous, quiet methodology[4]. He did not merely dig for treasure; he paid attention to stratigraphy, the principle that deeper layers of soil correspond to older periods of human occupation.

As Sahni cut his trenches into the mounds, he discovered that despite the horrific destruction caused by nineteenth-century railway quarrying, deeply buried architectural levels remained remarkably intact[1][10]. He uncovered seven to eight successive occupation levels built entirely of well-fired, durable red bricks[10]. Within these houses and paved streets, he collected rectangular steatite seals engraved with distinct animal figures and an unknown pictographic script[8][11].

Crucially, Sahni recognised that these levels contained no traces of iron, no Buddhist relics, and no coins of any historical dynasty[10][11]. The sheer absence of historical material convinced him that Harappa belonged to a pre-Mauryan, non-Buddhist epoch[10][11]. Sahni had uncovered the physical skeleton of an ancient metropolis, but without comparable evidence elsewhere, it remained a puzzle suspended in time.

The Buddhist Mound That Hid a Metropolis

Four hundred miles to the south, in the Larkana district of Sindh, another mound rose above the flood plains of the Indus River[2]. Locals called it Mohenjo-daro, meaning 'the mound of the dead men' in Sindhi[2]. Perched atop the highest point of the mound stood the ruins of a second-century CE Buddhist stupa dating to the Kushan era[2].

In the winter of 1921–1922, Rakhaldas Banerji, a fiery and unconventional superintendent in the ASI, arrived to excavate the Buddhist monument[2][7][8]. Banerji was an accomplished epigraphist with an intuitive eye for material culture[2]. As his excavators cleared the soil beneath the Kushan-era brickwork, they struck stone scrapers, unusual miniature vessels, and fired bricks far superior in composition to the Kushan masonry above them[2][8].

Then came the crucial breakthrough. Deep below the Buddhist floor, Banerji recovered steatite seals bearing an engraved bull and enigmatic symbols[8][11]. He immediately recalled Alexander Cunningham’s published drawings of the strange seals from Harappa[11]. Banerji realised he was standing on something vastly older than a Buddhist shrine. He observed that the entire mound was in fact a buried city of tremendous scale, laid out along broad avenues fringed by dense residential blocks[10].

In letters to Marshall written between 1922 and 1923, Banerji insisted that Mohenjo-daro possessed a 'remote antiquity'[11]. He argued that the structures under his spades belonged to a completely prehistoric culture that had flourished centuries before any known historic kingdom of northern India[11].

Marshall's Synthesis: Connecting the Dots

Finding strange ruins in one place is intriguing; finding matching ruins six hundred kilometres away is a historical revolution. That synthesis was the work of John Marshall[11]. By 1923, Marshall sat at his headquarters reviewing reports and crates of artefacts sent up from Punjab by Sahni and from Sindh by Banerji[11]. Other officers, including Madho Sarup Vats, confirmed that the seals, pottery forms, and building techniques matched precisely[1][11].

Marshall took a step that defines good scientific practice. He ordered the artefacts from Harappa and Mohenjo-daro brought together in one room and summoned Sahni and Banerji to examine them side by side[11]. The comparison left no room for doubt. The standardised baked bricks, the black-on-red painted ceramics, the chert blades, and above all, the square steatite seals incised with identical scripts belonged to the very same civilisation[8][11].

This was not an isolated frontier settlement. It was a single, coherent urban culture spanning a territory larger than Bronze Age Egypt or Mesopotamia. Marshall recognised that this discovery would change Indian historiography forever[7][10]. However, the team faced a stubborn dilemma: because the Indus script could not be read, they had no internal way of knowing whether these cities were 2,500 years old, 3,500 years old, or even older[9][12].

Marshall took a calculated gamble. He decided to publish the preliminary findings in an illustrated international magazine, sharing the mystery with the global scholarly community and asking for help to date the new world[9][12].

September 1924: A Forgotten Age Revealed

On 20 September 1924, John Marshall published a lavishly illustrated feature in The Illustrated London News under the bold headline: 'A Forgotten Age Revealed'[9][10][12]. He wrote plainly of the grandeur of the discoveries, describing massive brick houses, modern-looking drainage conduits, and sophisticated crafts[8][10]. He noted that just as Heinrich Schliemann had brought Mycenae to light, the ASI stood on the threshold of uncovering an unsuspected civilisation in the Indus plains[10].

The response was electrifying and immediate. Readers and specialists from across Europe wrote back[9][12]. Within weeks, Assyriologists and excavators working in Iraq—including Ernest Mackay, who was excavating at Kish in ancient Sumer—pointed out that nearly identical square seals and etched carnelian beads had been recovered in early Mesopotamian strata[1].

This cross-dating was decisive. Because the Mesopotamian layers were independently dated through cuneiform king lists, finding Indus artefacts in third-millennium BCE Sumerian contexts meant that Harappa and Mohenjo-daro were equally ancient[1]. The cities had traded with Mesopotamian metropolises during the third millennium BCE[1]. Indian history was not a late chapter in human urbanism. South Asia had possessed an independent, highly organised Bronze Age civilisation right alongside the Pharaohs and the Sumerians[1][8].

Beyond the Indus Valley

The discovery in 1924 was only the opening chapter. In the decades that followed, large-scale excavations directed by Marshall, Ernest Mackay, Madho Sarup Vats, and later Sir Mortimer Wheeler revealed the staggering sophistication of Mohenjo-daro and Harappa[1][7][8]. Yet the name first given to the culture—the 'Indus Valley Civilisation'—soon began to show its geographical limits[3].

When India was partitioned in 1947, both major urban centres, Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, fell within the borders of Pakistan[2][3]. Many colonial scholars, including Wheeler, assumed that Pakistan had inherited the sole cradle of this civilisation, leaving the newly independent Indian republic with almost no major sites[3]. Only two peripheral outposts, Rupar in Punjab and Rangpur in Gujarat, were initially known within India[3].

Indian archaeologists set out to test this assumption. In October 1950, a small team led by Amalananda Ghosh began extensive desert explorations across northern Rajasthan[3]. Ghosh mapped over one hundred ancient mounds, discovering twenty-five Harappan settlements along the dry, ancient riverbeds of the Ghaggar-Hakra system[3].

Ghosh showed that this culture was never confined to the banks of the Indus[3]. As later excavations at Kalibangan, Lothal, Dholavira, and Rakhigarhi proved, this urban phenomenon stretched from the foothills of the Himalayas to the Arabian Sea[1][3]. It was, as archaeologists now prefer to call it, the Harappan Civilisation—a continental web of trade, precision, and civic order whose discovery began with three men piecing together the clues of two forgotten mounds[1][3].

How do we know? · the historian's workshop

Who deserves primary credit for discovering the Indus Civilisation?

The evidence, step by step

1.
Daya Ram Sahni first excavated deeply stratified prehistoric brick buildings and seals at Harappa in 1920–1921[7][8][10].
2.
Rakhaldas Banerji independently unearthed identical seals beneath a Kushan stupa at Mohenjo-daro in 1921–1922 and recognised their prehistoric antiquity[2][8][11].
3.
John Marshall synthesised both discoveries, cross-referenced them with global Bronze Age sites, and financed the institutional campaigns[7][9][11].
∴
The discovery was not a solitary colonial breakthrough, but a collaborative synthesis where Indian field discoveries provided the critical material that Marshall coordinated and verified.

Colonial-era reporting routinely attributed the unearthing of ancient civilisations to high-ranking European administrators[2][8]. John Marshall indeed provided the administrative umbrella, the funding, and the crucial instinct to compare both sites together[7][8][11]. However, the physical intuition, field identification, and initial dating arguments came from Daya Ram Sahni and Rakhaldas Banerji[2][7][11]. The primary limitation of the archival record is that colonial administrative hierarchies tended to submerge Indian field officers' personal diaries and notes into official annual reports. Historians such as Nayanjot Lahiri have painstakingly reconstructed how much field deduction occurred before Marshall ever visited the trenches[2]. Understanding this discovery requires crediting both the sharp eyes of the Indian excavators on the ground and Marshall's administrative coordination.

Close reading

“Not often has it been given to archaeologists, as it was to Schliemann at Tiryns and Mycenae, or to Stein in the deserts of Turkestan, to light upon the remains of a long-forgotten civilisation. It looks, however, at this moment, as if we are on the threshold of such a discovery in the plains of the Indus.”

Sir John Marshall, The Illustrated London News, 20 September 1924[10]

Writing for an educated British and international public, Marshall chose his comparisons with immense care[10]. By invoking Heinrich Schliemann—the excavator who confirmed the historical reality of Homeric Troy and Mycenae—Marshall was telling the world that this was not a run-of-the-mill provincial find[10]. He was announcing the entry of an entirely new civilisation onto the global stage. Marshall highlighted the work of his Indian colleagues Sahni and Banerji inside the body of the article, but the dramatic public spotlight inevitably settled on Marshall himself[2][8][10]. The passage captures that pivotal moment when Indian antiquity ceased to be seen as late or derivative, claiming an equal footing with the most ancient societies on Earth.

How historians read it

Historical opinion has evolved through three distinct phases. In the 1920s and 1930s, contemporary British accounts hailed Marshall as the lone visionary who brought Mohenjo-daro to light[2][8]. Following Indian independence, nationalist historiography pushed back, highlighting how Rakhaldas Banerji was sidelined and forced out of the ASI due to administrative friction and racial bias[2]. In recent decades, balanced archival studies by historians like Nayanjot Lahiri show a more nuanced reality: Banerji had serious administrative disputes with his superiors, but his archaeological brilliance was unquestioned[2]. Marshall is now seen not as a solitary discoverer, but as an enlightened colonial administrator who championed Indian talent while remaining an agent of imperial institutions[8].

The strongest objection

Some critics argue that John Marshall deserves nearly all practical credit because Daya Ram Sahni and R. D. Banerji only carried out delegated field orders. In this view, neither Indian excavator understood the full civilisational scale until Marshall compared the seals, secured emergency government budgets, and cross-referenced the Mesopotamian evidence.

The best reply

Official correspondence directly refutes this claim. In 1923, before Marshall had even visited Mohenjo-daro, Banerji wrote to him explicitly diagnosing its 'remote antiquity' and noting that its seals matched Harappa[11]. Sahni had already established the non-Buddhist, prehistoric stratigraphy at Harappa[10][11]. Marshall synthesised the findings, but the core intellectual deduction happened in the field.

Fact, interpretation & debate

Stratigraphic excavation at Harappa and Mohenjo-daro conclusively established that both sites belonged to an identical Bronze Age urban culture[1][8][11]. Synchronism with Mesopotamian strata at Kish and Ur firmly dated this culture to the third millennium BCE[1][10]. Historians debate the distribution of credit between Director-General John Marshall and Indian excavators Daya Ram Sahni and Rakhaldas Banerji; colonial reports highlighted Marshall, while personal archives reveal that Banerji and Sahni diagnosed the prehistoric antiquity of the sites first[2][11]. Whether the uniform culture was governed by a centralised empire, regional city-states, or merchant oligarchies remains educated guesswork, as the Indus script remains undeciphered[9][12].

Why it matters now

Contemporary connections

The unearthing of the Harappan world continues to shape South Asian identity, urban policy, and border politics. When Partition divided British India in 1947, both flagship cities—Harappa and Mohenjo-daro—fell within Pakistan[2][3]. This loss provoked intense anxiety among Indian scholars, who refused to accept that India possessed no major Bronze Age heritage[3]. The resulting desert explorations led by Amalananda Ghosh revealed dozens of sites along the dried Ghaggar-Hakra riverbed, eventually leading to excavations at Kalibangan, Lothal, Dholavira, and Rakhigarhi[1][3].

Beyond geopolitics, the cities speak directly to modern municipal governance. As rapidly expanding cities like Bengaluru, Mumbai, and Delhi grapple with clogged storm drains and haphazard urban sprawls, the Harappan achievement stands out sharply. Harappan builders organised subterranean drains, inspection traps, and standardised burnt-brick domestic plumbing over four thousand years ago[8][10]. They solved civic hygiene at a scale that many modern municipalities still struggle to maintain.

Finally, Harappa serves as a cautionary tale of infrastructure destroying heritage. In the nineteenth century, British contractors systematically pulverised ancient walls to supply ballast for the Lahore-Multan railway[1]. Today, rapid highway construction, metro rail projects, and urban mining across India face the identical dilemma: protecting buried history while building modern connectivity.

In practice

Practical applications

Understanding how the Indus civilisation was uncovered offers practical lessons in problem-solving and institutional dynamics. First, it demonstrates the power of pattern recognition. John Marshall succeeded not because he dug the dirt himself, but because he brought finds from Harappa and Mohenjo-daro together, spotting the identical brick dimensions and seal scripts across hundreds of kilometres[8][11]. In any modern organisation, synthesis across silos is what turns isolated discoveries into breakthroughs.

Second, it teaches a sharp lesson about institutional credit. Ground-level work is often obscured by bureaucratic hierarchies; just as Sahni and Banerji were long overshadowed by colonial leadership, teams today must actively document and advocate for the contributors behind significant technical advances[2][8]. Finally, the Harappan emphasis on standardisation—from brick proportions to accurate stone weights—reminds professionals that scalable systems rely on universal baseline standards.

In one breath

Between 1920 and 1924, Daya Ram Sahni, Rakhaldas Banerji, and John Marshall connected distant mounds in Punjab and Sindh to reveal an urban Bronze Age world. By linking their finds to ancient Mesopotamia, they shattered the colonial myth of a late-starting India and pushed South Asian history back two thousand years.

Words worth owning

Stratigraphy — The study of layered rock or soil deposits to determine the relative chronological age of buried structures and artefacts.
“By tracking stratigraphy carefully, Daya Ram Sahni proved that Harappa's brick structures lay far below historical layers.”

Steatite — A soft, talc-rich metamorphic rock easily carved and hardened by firing, used widely by Harappans to produce seals.
“Engraved steatite seals discovered in Sindh and Punjab provided the first physical proof that both cities shared a single writing system.”

Cross-dating — A method of estimating the age of an archaeological layer by identifying imported artefacts from another culture whose chronology is already known.
“Cross-dating Indus seals found in Sumerian cities allowed scholars to place Harappa securely in the third millennium BCE.”

History is rarely discovered in a single flash of genius; it emerges when patient field observations are connected across time and space. The mounds of Harappa and Mohenjo-daro were always there, waiting for eyes curious enough to ask why two piles of red earth hundreds of miles apart told the exact same story.
